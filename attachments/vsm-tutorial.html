Value Stream Mapping

Learn to map how material and information move through a factory, from supplier to customer. Work through eight steps on a bracket plant, then replace the example numbers with your own.

Step 1 of 8

Pick a product family and find takt time

A value stream is every action, useful or not, that a product needs to get from raw material to the customer. Map one product family at a time: products that move through the same processing steps and share equipment. A single map of a whole plant becomes unreadable.

Customer demand and takt time

Takt time is the pace the customer sets: how often you must finish one piece to meet demand. Use the customer's real daily demand, not what the plant happens to make.

takt time = available working time per day ÷ customer demand per day

Step 2 of 8

Walk the flow and list the process steps

Take a clipboard and walk the real route of the product, door to door. Start at shipping and work upstream. This keeps you close to the customer, and it lets you see the last step first, where the pull begins.

Draw a process box wherever material stops flowing continuously and waits, or where a different team or machine takes over. One box can cover several machines if the parts pass straight from one to the next.

  • Draw boxes from left to right in the direction material moves.
  • Walk the floor instead of trusting the routing sheet. Rework loops and detours only show up on foot.
  • Add the supplier on the left and the customer on the right, with the trucks that connect them.

Put the walk in order

Step 3 of 8

Collect data for each process

Stand at each process and fill in a data box underneath it. A handful of numbers is enough to judge whether the process can keep up with the customer.

C/T
Cycle time: seconds from one finished piece to the next.
C/O
Changeover time: minutes lost switching from one product to another.
Uptime
Share of scheduled time the machine is actually able to run.
Ops
Number of operators needed to run the process.

On the floor

Time several cycles yourself with a stopwatch and use a typical value. Standard times in the planning system usually describe how the process should run, not how it does.

Step 4 of 8

Count the inventory

Wherever parts sit and wait, draw a triangle with an I inside. Count what is physically there: raw material, work in progress between processes, and finished goods. Then turn each count into days of stock so the numbers are comparable.

days of stock = pieces in stock ÷ customer demand per day

Step 5 of 8

Draw the information flow

Above the material flow, show how each process finds out what to make and when. Forecasts, orders, schedules and shouted instructions all count. The box that sends them is production control.

Use a dashed line for each flow and write the frequency on it. Add the flows one at a time, the way the supervisor would describe them.

Add the flows to the map

On the floor

Ask the supervisor how they know what to run next. Draw what they say first, then compare it with what you see on the floor. The two often differ.

Step 6 of 8

Mark push or pull and draw the timeline

A striped arrow marks push: a process makes parts to its own schedule and sends them on whether or not the next process needs them. Under a pull system, the next process asks for parts when it uses them. Every striped arrow on this map is a place where stock can pile up.

Then draw the timeline ladder along the bottom. Upper steps show how long parts wait in each stock. Lower steps show how long a part is worked on. Add them up to get the production lead time and the value-adding time.

Step 7 of 8

Read the map: takt, bottleneck and lead time

Now compare each process with the customer. Divide cycle time by uptime to get the time a good piece really takes, and set it against takt. A process above the line cannot meet demand, and the slowest one limits the whole flow.

Process time against takt

Effective time = C/T ÷ uptime. Bars turn amber when a process uses more than 90% of takt.

Step 8 of 8

Find the waste and draw the future state

The map shows waste as stock, not as slow machines. Choose improvements that move the plant toward continuous flow where parts move one at a time, and toward pull where flow is not yet possible. Each idea you switch on adds a starburst to the map and shrinks the stock it targets.
